Explaining an excess return model using a company's financials.
The model values JPM’s common equity directly: what the bank is worth is its tangible book value plus the present value of the returns it earns above what shareholders require. It’s called an excess-return or residual-income model.

The core idea

If a bank earns exactly its cost of equity on its book, it’s worth book value, because extra growth adds nothing. If it earns more, it’s worth a premium, and the size of the premium depends on how large the excess return is and how long it lasts.

Equity value = TCE₀ + Σ PV of residual income + PV of terminal residual income
Residual income (year t) = (ROTCE_t − cost of equity) × TCE at start of year t
Step by step, with your JPM numbers

Starting point (TCE₀ = $288.36B). Tangible common equity is stockholders’ equity minus preferred stock, goodwill and other intangibles. It’s the FY2025 balance.
Starting ROTCE (20.9%). Return on tangible common equity is net income to common divided by average TCE. The model averages the last three years, which gives 20.9%.
Cost of equity (9.8%). CAPM: 4.2% risk-free rate plus beta × a 5.5% equity risk premium. Beta comes from yfinance. A bank’s debt is mostly operating funding, so there’s no WACC and no net-debt subtraction.
Fade assumptions. ROTCE glides linearly from 20.9% to a terminal 15.3% (the midpoint of starting ROTCE and the cost of equity) over 10 years. Book growth glides from 5.5% (the historical TCE trend) down to 2.5% terminal growth.
Each forecast year:
Net income = ROTCE × beginning TCE. For 2026 that’s 20.92% × $288.36B = $60.33B.
Residual income = net income − 9.8% × beginning TCE = $60.33B − $28.26B ≈ $32.1B.
Ending TCE = beginning TCE × (1 + book growth) = $304.33B.
Implied payout = net income − growth in TCE = $44.37B (73.5% of earnings). This is whatever isn’t retained, dividends plus buybacks, so it’s an output, not an input.
Discount that year’s residual income at 9.8%.
Terminal value. After year 10, ROTCE stays at 15.35% and book grows at 2.5% forever:
   TV = (15.35% − 9.8%) × $427.4B ÷ (9.8% − 2.5%) ≈ $325B  → PV ≈ $128.5B
Add up: $288.36B (book) + $184.41B (PV of years 1-10) + $128.54B (terminal) = $601.31B of common equity. Dividing by 2.66B shares gives $226.21 per share.
Why this suits a bank

Book and ROTCE are the clean inputs: they’re stable, well reported and tied to regulatory capital, unlike operating cash flow, which swings with loans, deposits and trading assets.
Payout isn’t an input: growth in tangible book is set directly, so payout falls out (retention = growth ÷ ROTCE). That keeps ROTCE, growth and payout consistent. You can sanity-check the implied 74-84% against the historical 65% average.
Current share count: value per share divides by today’s shares, not an average of forecast shares. Buybacks are already inside the payout, so dividing by a shrinking count would count them twice.
What drives the answer

Terminal ROTCE and cost of equity: these move the result most. The $128B terminal piece is 21% of value, and the 10-year PV is another 31%.
The market gap: the market’s 3.13x P/TBV vs your 2.09x says investors expect JPM to hold a ROTCE near or above 20-25% for much longer than a fade to 15%, or they use a lower cost of equity (around 8.4%).
Limitations

Book value is FY2025 against a June 2026 share count, so about nine months of retained earnings are missing.
The model doesn’t separately forecast credit losses, rate sensitivity or regulatory capital requirements. It captures them only through the ROTCE path.
Beta and the 5.5% equity risk premium come from market data and a fixed assumption. They’re the weakest inputs, so use the sliders to see how value moves.
